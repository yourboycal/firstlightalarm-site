Does the 5am club work? What the science says about early rising

First Light · 10 September 2026 · 4 min read

Robin Sharma's The 5AM Club has sold millions of copies on a simple promise: wake at five, spend the first hour on twenty minutes of movement, twenty of reflection and twenty of learning, and the rest of your life improves [1]. It is a good story. Some of it is also good science. The question is which parts.

What holds up

The first hour matters. The book's central move, protecting the first hour of the day before anyone else can claim it, is sound. Habits form by repetition in a stable context, and the hour after waking is the most stable context most adults have. Nothing has happened yet. Nobody has emailed. The research on consistent exercise timing bears this out: in a study of 375 long-term weight loss maintainers, people who exercised at a consistent time did more of it, and nearly half of those with a consistent slot had chosen the early morning [5].

Movement first is the right order. Sharma's twenty minutes of exercise before anything else lines up with what we know about sleep inertia: judgement and memory are impaired for the first part of the morning, recovering over two to four hours [6]. Spending that window on something physical rather than something that needs a clear head is exactly right.

Consistency is the mechanism. Strip away the numerology and the book is a consistency programme: same time, same sequence, every day. That is what habit research says works.

What doesn't

The hour itself. There is no study showing that 5am does anything 6am or 7am cannot. What the research does show is that people differ, a lot, in when their bodies want to sleep and wake. Chronobiologist Till Roenneberg's work on chronotypes describes a spread of several hours across the population between natural early risers and natural late sleepers, and it is largely biological, not a matter of discipline [3].

Roenneberg's group coined a term for what happens when a late type lives on an early schedule: social jetlag, the gap between the time your body clock wants and the time your alarm demands. In their 2006 study, later chronotypes showed the largest differences in sleep timing between work days and free days and accumulated a considerable sleep debt on work days, with the mismatch associated with worse wellbeing and heavier use of stimulants [2]. A night owl who joins the 5am club without moving bedtime is not becoming disciplined. They are becoming sleep deprived on a schedule.

A 5am alarm is not a habit. A 9:30pm bedtime is the habit. The alarm is just where it shows up.

The survivorship. Early risers who succeed are visible and get asked about their routines. Early risers who burned out, and successful people who sleep until eight, are less often profiled. That is not evidence against early rising. It is a reason not to treat the anecdotes as evidence for it.

The maths nobody does

Most adults need somewhere around seven to nine hours. A 5am alarm with eight hours of sleep means lights out at nine. With seven and a half, 9:30. If your evenings currently end at eleven, the 5am club is not asking you to wake earlier. It is asking you to give up your evenings, and that is the commitment to evaluate honestly before buying an alarm clock.

If the evenings cannot move, the wake time should not either. A consistent 6:45 with a real first hour will outperform a 5am that lasts eleven days.

If you want to become a morning person anyway

It is possible, and the lever is not the alarm. It is light. Kenneth Wright's team at the University of Colorado sent people camping for a week with only natural light, no screens, no electric bulbs, and found their body clocks shifted about two hours earlier, with the biological night ending before they woke instead of after [4]. You do not need a tent. You need bright light as early as possible and less of it late.

  1. Fix bedtime first. Move it fifteen minutes earlier each week. The wake time follows; it cannot lead.
  2. Get outside within ten minutes of waking. Daylight, even overcast, is many times brighter than indoor lighting. This is what moves the clock.
  3. Dim the evenings. Screens and bright rooms after nine push the clock later and undo the morning's work.
  4. Keep the wake time at weekends. A Saturday lie-in is a two-hour flight east every week. This is the step most people skip and the reason most early-rising attempts fail.
  5. Put one physical mission in the first ten minutes. Steps, push-ups, a walk to the corner. The alarm gets you awake; the mission gets you up.
  6. Judge it at 3pm, not 5am. The test of an earlier schedule is how you feel in the afternoon after three weeks, not how heroic the alarm looks.

The verdict

The 5am club works for morning chronotypes and for anyone willing to move their whole evening two hours earlier. For everyone else, it is a sleep-restriction programme with good branding. The parts worth keeping are the parts that have nothing to do with the number five: a fixed wake time, light on your face, something physical before anything cerebral, and the same sequence every day until it runs itself.

That is what First Light is for, at whatever hour you set it. The alarm rings, the mission you chose the night before is waiting, and the alarm does not stop until the mission is done. Whether the mission happens at 5:00 or 6:45 is between you and your bedtime.

Questions people ask

Is waking up at 5am healthy?

It is healthy if you are asleep by around 9:30pm and your body clock is comfortable there. If it is funded by cutting sleep to six hours, it is not, whatever the routine that follows looks like. The hour is neutral; the sleep is not.

Do successful people really wake at 5am?

Some do. Many do not, and they are less often interviewed about it. Early rising is easy to notice and easy to copy, which makes it look like a cause when it is often just a preference of people who happen to be morning types.

How do I become a morning person?

Move the wake time earlier in steps of fifteen minutes a week, move bedtime with it, and get bright light as soon as you wake. Light is the strongest lever there is on the body clock; a week of natural light shifts it about two hours earlier. Keep the same wake time at weekends or the shift will not hold.

Is 6am or 6:30 good enough?

Yes. Nothing in the research privileges 5am over any other hour. What the research supports is consistency, morning light, and doing something physical in the first minutes. All of that works at 6:30.
