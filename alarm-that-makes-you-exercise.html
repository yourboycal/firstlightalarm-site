Movement missions

The alarm that makes you exercise before it stops.

Set a push-up or squat mission tonight. Tomorrow the alarm rings, you prop the phone against the wall, and the countdown only runs while the camera can see you moving. Stop, and it waits. Finish, and the morning is yours.

Why an exercise alarm works when willpower doesn't

The decision to snooze is not made by you. It is made by a version of you in the first minutes after waking, when reaction time, memory and judgement are all impaired by what sleep researchers call sleep inertia. Asking that person to choose the gym over the duvet is a losing bet, which is why a 2025 study of three million nights found the snooze button pressed on 56% of all mornings.

An alarm you have to exercise to turn off changes the shape of the problem. There is no decision to make at 6:30. The decision was made last night, by the version of you that could be trusted with it. All the morning version has to do is get on the floor, and thirty seconds later sleep inertia has lifted, the blood is moving, and the hardest part of the day is already behind you.

How First Light verifies it

For push-ups and squats, First Light uses the front camera as a movement sensor. Prop the phone against a wall or a stack of books so it can see you, and start. The countdown runs only while the camera can see you moving. Pause, and the numbers pause with you.

It is deliberately simple. Each frame is shrunk to a coarse grid of brightness values, compared with the previous one, and thrown away. Nothing is recorded, nothing is uploaded, and there is no account or server on the other end. The app is not recognising your body or counting reps. It is checking one thing: are you in motion?

That simplicity is also what makes it fair. It works in a dim room, in pyjamas, at any angle where the camera can see a person moving. And if it genuinely cannot see you, it does not trap you: after about twenty seconds without movement it hands over to a plain timer, and there is always a button to run the timer instead.

Setting it up takes a minute

  1. Pick the mission. Add an alarm, choose Push-ups or Squats from the Movement list, and set the time.
  2. Set the length in seconds. Thirty is a real mission. Sixty is a solid one. You can raise it any night.
  3. Choose what to log afterwards. Reps, time, or nothing. Yesterday's number sits beside the field so you can see the trend without opening a spreadsheet.
  4. Grant camera access when the app asks during setup, so tomorrow morning has no dialogs to get through.

In the morning: the alarm rings through Silent mode and Focus, you tap it, prop the phone, and go. When the countdown reaches zero the alarm is done and the app asks how it went.

What counts as done

You set the floor, and the floor should be low. The point of the mission is not fitness, at least not at first. It is to make getting up the path of least resistance and staying down slightly annoying. Thirty seconds of push-ups clears sleep inertia and puts a win on the board before you have had a coherent thought. The fitness follows from doing that two hundred mornings in a row, which is far more likely if the daily ask is small.

A note on bad mornings. Every timed mission has a floor of a couple of minutes and every movement mission hands over to a timer if the camera cannot see you. The alarm is strict about starting and forgiving about form. Nobody is watching. There is no score for technique.

The other movement missions

  • Morning steps. Counted live by the phone's pedometer. A hundred steps gets you to the kettle; five hundred gets you round the block. More on the step mission.
  • Yoga. A timed session that begins with a photo of your mat, then a timer you cannot skip.
  • Handstand practice. Timed, against a wall, with a photo of the wall to start.
  • Build your own. Name it, choose camera-verified or timed, set the length. Burpees, skipping, a plank: anything the camera can see you do.

Then stack the next habit on it

Once the exercise mission has run three mornings in a row, First Light starts offering a second mission the moment you finish the first: meditation after the push-ups, or a page of your journal. Say yes once and it is saved as "Push-ups, then Meditation" every morning. Say not now and it asks again another day. A stacked mission is always optional and never brings the alarm back. It is the mechanism behind habit stacking, built into the one habit you will never forget to perform: waking up.

Progress you can actually see

Every morning won adds to your level, which never resets. The Progress tab charts your best set over the last month, your streak, and the share of scheduled mornings you have won. Ranks land on the milestones from habit research: seven mornings, thirty, and sixty-six, the average time a habit takes to become automatic.

Questions people ask

Does the alarm really not stop until I exercise?

Tapping Stop quiets it for ninety seconds, then it comes back, five more times across ten minutes. The only thing that ends it is finishing the mission you set the night before. There is no snooze.

How does it know I am doing push-ups?

The front camera watches for movement. Each frame is reduced to a coarse brightness grid on the phone, compared with the last one, and discarded. If the grid is changing, you are moving and the countdown runs. If it stops changing, the countdown pauses. It is not counting reps or recognising your body; it is checking that you are in motion.

Is it recording me?

No. Nothing is saved and nothing leaves the phone. There is no server to send it to. You can see the live camera view on screen so you know exactly what it can see.

What if the camera cannot see me?

If it cannot detect movement for around twenty seconds it hands over to a plain timer, and there is always a button to just run the timer. You will never be trapped by bad light or a badly propped phone.

How long should I set?

Whatever you will actually do on your worst morning. Thirty seconds of push-ups is a real mission. Most people start there and raise it once the habit has stuck.

Which exercises can it verify?

Push-ups and squats use the camera. Morning steps are counted by the phone's pedometer. Yoga and handstand practice are timed missions that begin with a photo of your mat or your wall.

Do I need an Apple Watch or any equipment?

No. Just an iPhone running iOS 26 and something to lean it against.
