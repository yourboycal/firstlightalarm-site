The snooze button is procrastination in its purest form: a small, pleasant delay of a thing you have already decided to do. Most of what follows the alarm has the same shape. The phone before the shower, the shower before the task, the task after one more coffee. The morning is where procrastination lives, and the research on why is unusually clear.

What predicts procrastination, and why mornings have all of it

Piers Steel's 2007 meta-analysis pooled 691 correlations from the procrastination literature and found four strong, consistent predictors: task aversiveness (how unpleasant it feels), task delay (how far off the payoff is), low self-efficacy (how little you believe you can do it), and impulsiveness (how easily a nearer pleasure wins) [1]. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation-seeking barely mattered. Procrastination is not a personality flaw. It is a predictable response to a task that feels bad now and pays off later, in the presence of something that feels good now.

Now look at the first hour of the day through that lens. Every task feels more aversive, because sleep inertia impairs mood, memory and judgement for the first part of the morning, and Megan Jewett's team found the recovery curve does not flatten for two to four hours [2]. Every payoff feels further away, because the day has not started. Self-efficacy is at its daily low, because you cannot think straight yet and you know it. And the most impulsive alternative available to a human being, the phone, is within arm's reach of the bed. The morning is not a hard time to start because you are weak. It is a hard time to start because all four dials are turned up.

Procrastination is a task that feels bad now, a reward that feels far away, and something nearer that feels better. At 6:45, that describes everything.

Fix one: shrink the task until it is not aversive

David Allen's two-minute rule, from Getting Things Done, says that if a task takes less than two minutes, do it now rather than filing it [5]. James Clear turned it into a rule for starting: scale any new habit down to a two-minute version, and do that [6]. Both work on the first dial in Steel's list. A two-minute version of a task is less aversive than the whole task, and it turns out that most tasks, once started, are less aversive than they looked from outside. "Write the report" is procrastinated. "Open the document and write one sentence" is done, and then usually followed by the second sentence.

For mornings, the two-minute version of almost any habit is the whole habit, at first. Thirty seconds of push-ups. One page. Three hundred steps. The size is the point. You are not training the body; you are training the start.

Fix two: decide when and where, the night before

Peter Gollwitzer's research on implementation intentions is the most robust finding in the planning literature: a plan of the form "when X, I will Y" roughly doubles the rate at which people actually do Y, compared with intending to [3]. The morning is the ideal place to apply it, because it takes the decision away from the groggy version of you and hands it to last night's version, who was capable of making it.

There is a bonus. E.J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that unfinished tasks intrude on your thinking, and that making a specific plan for them eliminated the intrusion even before anything was done [4]. Writing down tomorrow's first task tonight quietens tonight as well as starting tomorrow.

Fix three: move first, think second

Two of the four dials, aversiveness and self-efficacy, are inflated by sleep inertia, and sleep inertia fades with time and movement. So the first thing you do should be physical and brainless: stand up, walk, a short set of something. Not because it is productive but because it turns the dials down before you meet the real task. Trying to start cognitive work in the first ten minutes is the procrastination trap in its most reliable form: the task feels impossible, because for those ten minutes it nearly is.

Fix four: put the impulsive option further away than the task

Steel's fourth dial is impulsiveness, and the morning's impulsive option is the phone. A 2025 study of three million nights found the snooze button pressed on 56% of mornings [7]; the phone check that follows is the same reflex with a different app. The fix is distance. Phone across the room. Alarm that cannot be dismissed from bed. Book on the pillow, laptop closed. The winning move in an impulse contest is not to have the contest.

This is the mechanism First Light is built on. The alarm does not stop when you tap it; it stops when the mission you set last night is done, and the mission is physical. By the time it is finished, the first three fixes have happened without a single decision: the task was small, it was decided in advance, and you moved before you thought.

Fix five: make the reward nearer

The second dial, delay, is the hardest to move because the real payoff of most worthwhile things is weeks away. What you can do is add a near one. Log it. Tick it. Watch a streak grow or a number climb. This sounds trivial and it is not: it converts a task with a distant reward into a task with a same-morning one, and Steel's analysis says that is the difference between doing and delaying. It is why the app asks how the mission went and shows yesterday's number beside today's. The push-ups pay off in months. The log pays off now.

The morning, rebuilt

  1. Tonight: write tomorrow's one task, and the first physical thing you will do when the alarm rings.
  2. Alarm: the physical thing, immediately, before the phone. Small enough to be undeniable.
  3. Log it. The near reward.
  4. Then the two-minute version of the real task: open the file, write the sentence, send the one message.
  5. Only then the inbox, the news, the feed. They will keep.

None of this requires feeling ready. That is the point. Steel's four dials describe why you will not feel ready in the morning, probably ever. The fixes all work by starting anyway, at a size and in an order that does not need the feeling.
